    Kinstead

    A multiplayer castle game that runs in the browser, designed and built in about two days by one person directing a team of AI agents.

    This is the story of how we got there: the mockups, the art we threw away, what we learned about character design and Grok, and the engineering that lets 30 people play at once.

    Sulla answered with three clickable mockups, one per ingredient, and a working name: Kinstead, “the place where kin stand”.

    Art direction

    16-bit, 32-bit, 64-bit or 128-bit?

    We rendered the same village in different console-era styles so the only thing being compared was the look.

    “i want the style to be more like runescape though, what you gave me has absolutely no detail in the characters or scene, so its not very exciting”Jonathon, reacting to an early blocky version
    What we learnedDetail is what makes people excited. The fix was characters built from many pieces (faces, beards, helmets, capes, tools) and hand-painted textures on every surface.

    Source: mockups 07–09 and the hackathon conversation.

    The world

    A snowy ruin on a cliff, and the size of a real RTS map

    Jonathon shared a hand-painted battlemap as a style reference only: snowy ruins on a cliff plateau with one bridge in. We painted our own maps in that spirit rather than copying it.

    “you lost the whole look and feel of the map. you have got to get closer to this rendering”Jonathon, on the first attempt

    The fix was a new renderer: every pixel of terrain painted from a height field (layered rock strata, light from the top left, ink rims on cliff edges), then a hand-drawn detail layer of snow drifts, rubble and ferns on top.

    Source: hackathon and map-builder conversations, 13 Nov; mockups 10–20.

    What we learnedStudy what makes famous characters work, then design your own. The principles are free to use; the characters are not.

    Characters, part 2

    What makes a character design effective in a game

    Five rules we kept coming back to, each one learned by looking at a render and seeing it fail.

    1 · A readable silhouette

    Chunky shapes you recognise from the outline alone. Our 16-bit set was “silhouette-led”: big heads, a 1 px near-black outline, four flat tones.

    2 · A signature prop per role

    Knight: flame tabard and shield. Ranger: hood and bow. Mage: glowing staff. Troll: huge club. If two heroes share a shape, players mix them up.

    3 · Contrast with the world

    Heroes must separate from snow and rock: crisp, high-contrast, a contact shadow under every figure, and sharp inside the camera's focus band.

    4 · Judge it from the game camera

    A character that looks great in a close-up can turn to mush at play distance. Every candidate was checked in the actual world view.

    5 · Enough detail to be exciting

    Faces, armour pieces, straps, tools. “No detail” was the first and loudest piece of feedback.

    6 · It has to animate

    A still that reads well can still fall apart in a walk or attack cycle. We learned that one the hard way (see the 3D slides).

    Source: mockups 12, 21, 22; the character review in the asset conversation, 14 Nov.

    Characters, part 3

    Pixel heroes in a 3D miniature

    Characters, part 4 · Grok

    Grok Imagine became our concept artist

    Jonathon generated hero concepts in Grok Imagine and picked his favourites. Those four became the cast: the knight is the player, the ranger and mage are friendly kin, and the frost troll is the enemy.

    Voxel HD

    Painted HD

    What we learnedGrok was great at inspiration: in minutes it produced heroes with clear silhouettes, signature props and a palette that matched the snowy world. Our agents couldn't call Grok's image generation from their runtime, so this step happened in Jonathon's own signed-in Grok tab, with the agents doing the cutting out and wiring up.

    From concept to 3D

    Can a game look exactly like the Grok art, from every angle?

    The game camera orbits, so a flat picture isn't enough. We ran a fidelity spike on two pilots, the knight and the woodcutter's lodge, with four approaches.

    The same four, from behind

    What we learnedNo tested option earned “exactly like Grok at every angle.” Adding more cubes doesn't recreate the art either: resolution alone is not enough.

    Source: 28-fidelity-spike-report.md and its 32 captured renders.

    From concept to 3D

    Grok's turnarounds drift

    To lock the back and sides we asked Grok for turnaround sheets of the same object. It gave us something different instead: a plausible object, not the same one.

    • Prompted angles aren't reliable: several “side” and “back” views came back as three-quarter fronts.
    • Helmets, emblems and sigils simplify or change between panels.
    • Each panel is about 448×504 px, far below the original concept.
    What we learnedGetting consistent views is the hardest step. A human has to approve every view before any 3D work starts.

    Source: fidelity spike report, “directly measured view consistency problems”; ASSET-PIPELINE.md finding 2.

    From concept to 3D · the lesson

    Nobody gets a faithful 3D model from one image

    Our hand-sculpted and reconstructed models failed because we tried to rebuild a painting by hand, or from a single guess of its depth. Jonathon confirmed the models don't have to be cubes, which opens the proven route:

    1 · ViewsFront, side, back and ¾ from the approved Grok concept. A human approves each one.
    2 · MeshA multi-image 3D generator turns the approved views into a textured mesh.
    3 · Faithful textureRe-project the original art from the matching camera, so the game view is the painting.
    4 · Clean upDecimate to a game budget (about 10–30k triangles a hero) and bake one texture atlas.
    5 · RigAuto-rig a clean humanoid mesh with idle, walk, run and attack.
    6 · CheckScore it in the Asset Lab from the game camera. Ship only what passes.

    Multiple views

    are the only documented way to lock the back and sides. Single-image tools invent them.

    Consistent views

    are the hardest part. Edit models that start from the approved art hold identity better than generic multi-view.

    Mesh + auto-rig

    is a solved path. Chunky voxel characters break auto-riggers.

    Next step before committing: a half-day, three-asset bake-off (knight, lodge, one prop) across two paid generators and one open model, scored in the Asset Lab.

    Source: ASSET-PIPELINE.md (15 Nov 2026), backed by the cited research in the asset-research folder.

    Game design

    Three design docs in one day

    Each plan was a written design with every number in a data file checked by a validator, so code and design never drifted apart.

    v0.1 · Hearth Level

    “Your home is your power”

    Light a hearth in a frozen frontier. Every room and piece of furniture raises your Hearth Level, which makes your hero stronger. Built on a study of how ten survival and base-building games link the base to the player's power (structure only, never their content).

    v1.0 · Simple Core

    Restoration and completion

    For players in their 30s to 50s: simple to play, no friction to start, and something visibly changes about every five minutes. Light the hearth, build your home, push back the frost.

    v1.1 · Castle

    Spend smart, guard your friends

    Jonathon's direction: economic strategy, a castle instead of a house, and growth for the social side. Every build card shows its payback (“+6 wood/min · pays for itself in 1.7 min”). The frost raids at dusk.

    “Rebuild a frozen castle. Spend smart so it pays you back. Guard it, and your friends', against the frost.”GAME-PLAN-CASTLE.md, page 1
    What we learnedThe AI pushed back where it mattered: a “pays back in 2 days” economy can't be felt in a 15-minute demo, so paybacks became minutes, and raids against friends stayed gentle (no destruction, loot caps, shields).

    Source: GAME-PLAN.md, GAME-PLAN-SIMPLE.md, GAME-PLAN-CASTLE.md (all 14 Nov 2026).

    Friday afternoon

    Make it feel good to play

    Jonathon took the controls himself and pushed a long run of changes straight from playing:

    • Diablo-style controls: hold to walk, one click for the main action, right-click menu.
    • The tool in your hand decides the click: felled trees, dug dirt and mined stone become pickups.
    • Build anywhere, drag to draw walls, hold to turn a building as you drop it.
    • Survival: a real cottage home, wolves and a thief, day and night, a door you can shut.
    • Wolves raid your food stores at night, gnawing swing by swing.
    • Walls and buildings between the camera and your hero fade see-through.
    What we learnedThe fastest design loop was the person with the vision playing the build and saying exactly what felt wrong. Agents turned each note into a small, tested commit.

    Source: commit history on feat/diablo-controls, 14 Nov.

    Engineering

    One room per map, on Cloudflare

    BrowserPlain JavaScript + Three.js, built with esbuild. No framework.
    Cloudflare PagesServes the static game. First load is about 525 KB (brotli).
    Workerkinstead-rooms routes /ws/<map> to that map's room.
    Durable ObjectOne GameRoom per map holds the world: players, plots, stores, doors, farms, teams.

    Plots, not chaos

    Each player gets their own plot on the shared map. When a map is full, the server recycles the plot of the player who has been away longest, but never a town whose owner was seen within a week.

    The server decides

    A trust pass made the server the owner of ownership and sync, so a client can't claim another player's buildings.

    OOP, one job per class

    The original single-file room was split into small services: PlayerRegistry, WorldState, MobRelay, FarmSync, PlotRecycler, Teams, Markets, RoomStats…

    Testable locally

    A local Durable Object server and a two-browser headless harness let agents play two players against each other before anything shipped.

    Source: server/src/ and server/wrangler.toml; PRs #8, #14, #28.

    Performance

    Making it smooth for 30 players

    We ran a live load test with 30 players on one map, found the bottleneck, fixed it, and ran it again.

    Messages per player
    15113.4/s
    11× fewer
    Bandwidth per player
    20624.4 KB/s
    8× less
    Ping, 95th percentile
    27960 ms
    4.6× faster

    What changed

    • Server: creature updates batched and sent 4 times a second, only to players in range; farms sync only what changed; resyncs throttled.
    • Client: only the nearest 60 remote creatures are drawn, reusing the same actors instead of creating new ones.

    Source: PR #25 “perf/thirty-players”; load-test numbers measured on 15 Nov.

    What we learned

    • Show, don't describe. Mockups and screenshots beat paragraphs for every decision.
    • Make agents look at what they built. Screenshot it, compare it to the reference, then report.
    • Ask for an honest verdict. “Dump it” on the 3D experiment saved hours.
    • Small parallel branches, merged in batches, then one integration test before deploy.
    • Test like a player. Two real browsers found bugs that unit tests never would.
